Self-Help in Today’s Leasing Market

When landlords fall behind on their obligations, a well-crafted self-help clause lets tenants fix critical issues and recoup the cost. Here are the five elements to push for.

By Endurance Real Estate Advisors [CONFIRM: author] · November 20, 2026 [CONFIRM: original publish date]

A self-help clause gives a commercial tenant the right to fix certain problems the landlord fails to address, then recover the cost, often through a rent offset. In today’s New York City office market, it is one of the most important protections a tenant can negotiate.

As a commercial real estate broker, I’ve seen how quickly things can unravel when landlords fall behind on their financial obligations and tenants don’t have the right protections in place.

Buildings once considered financially stable are now under distress. Imagine arriving at your office to find one of the elevators out of service for days, your space not cleaned the night before, or the air conditioning not working properly. If a property falls into receivership, the situation can deteriorate even further.

That’s why I strongly advise clients: whether negotiating a new office lease or a renewal, don’t overlook the importance of a well-crafted self-help clause.

What is a self-help clause?

A self-help clause gives tenants the right to step in and address a landlord’s failure to perform certain obligations, typically related to maintaining the leased premises, and recoup the cost, often through a rent offset.

It’s a safeguard for critical services tied directly to your space: HVAC servicing, plumbing repairs, lighting, janitorial services, and other operational necessities that go unaddressed despite repeated notices.

Why it matters right now in NYC

In New York City, many office landlords are under financial pressure, cutting operating budgets or reducing building services to stay afloat. Without a self-help clause, tenants have little recourse if garbage piles up, restrooms go uncleaned, or heating and cooling systems stop working.

In buildings where ownership is distracted by debt service or loan workouts, tenants often find themselves waiting or, worse, paying out of pocket with no reimbursement.

This isn’t theoretical. In today’s NYC market, I’ve seen firsthand how delays in basic services can hurt productivity, disrupt operations, and even lead to lost business.

What should a strong self-help clause include?

Based on years of experience structuring leases in New York’s office market, here are five elements every tenant should push for:

  1. Clear scope. The lease should specifically define which repairs or services tenants can address. Focus on non-structural issues affecting your premises.
  2. Defined notice and cure periods. Require written notice and a clear window for the landlord to respond, typically 10 to 30 days. Some leases require a second notice; make sure the timing is realistic.
  3. Reimbursement or offset mechanism. If you’re fixing something the landlord failed to handle, the lease should outline how and when you’re reimbursed, ideally via rent offset, without arbitrary dollar limits.
  4. Avoid rent withholding confusion. A self-help clause isn’t a free pass to stop paying rent. It should allow cost recovery, not blanket rent withholding, which could put you in default.
  5. Legal review. Work with a qualified real estate attorney familiar with NYC office leases. Language matters; these clauses must be tailored and enforceable.

What happens without it?

I’ve seen too many tenants trapped in underperforming buildings with no ability to act. In some cases, a lack of basic cleaning services or an HVAC or plumbing issue disrupted operations for weeks because the lease lacked a basic self-help right.

In today’s market, with more buildings being sold at distressed prices, services can slip. Without self-help protection, tenants are forced to wait or escalate disputes, often at high cost.

Bottom line: it’s a strategic must-have

In this market, with high vacancies, minimal rent growth, increased operating expenses and financially stressed landlords, the self-help clause isn’t just legal fine print. It’s a strategic safeguard.

Tenants need the ability to protect their operations. In a tenant-favorable environment like NYC’s, this clause is negotiable, and it should be part of every serious lease conversation. Don’t wait for something to go wrong to realize you don’t have the tools to fix it.
