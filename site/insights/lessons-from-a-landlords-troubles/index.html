Lessons From a Landlord’s Troubles

Reported defaults across a major NYC landlord’s portfolio show how ownership distress can shift leverage to tenants, and what due diligence to run before you sign.

Endurance Real Estate Advisors · November 20, 2026

When a landlord is in financial distress, tenants often gain negotiating leverage, but they also take on new risks that require careful due diligence and stronger lease protections. Recent headlines surrounding Joe Moinian’s real estate portfolio are more than industry gossip; they’re a window into today’s market and an opportunity for tenants to take control.

In the past few weeks alone:

  • LNR Partners filed suit to seize 535-545 Fifth Avenue, alleging default on a $310M loan.
  • AIG moved to foreclose on 500-512 Seventh Avenue, where $285M remains outstanding.
  • Moinian lost the Hilton Garden Inn in Midtown via UCC foreclosure after failing to pay off a $175M loan.

With interest rates still elevated and lenders growing less flexible, even long-established owners are finding it hard to refinance or restructure. This matters to you as a tenant because distress at the ownership level can change the equation when it comes to your lease.

Tenant advantage in a distressed market

Reports of high-profile defaults, including lawsuits, foreclosure filings, and ownership turnover, may seem like distant headlines. But if you’re a tenant in New York City, this kind of distress can directly affect your lease, your rent, and your future occupancy. Here’s how to think strategically, and how we help clients use this moment to their advantage.

Negotiating leverage

When landlords are under financial pressure, they’re far more likely to work with tenants to stabilize their income and avoid vacancies.

If you’re a current tenant, this could mean:

  • Opportunities to renegotiate lease terms
  • Lower rent or rent abatements
  • Early renewal options with better terms
  • Relief from escalations or pass-through charges

If you’re a prospective tenant looking for new space, distressed landlords may offer larger concession packages, including:

  • Generous free rent periods: often several months, significantly reducing initial occupancy costs.
  • Higher tenant improvement (TI) allowances: negotiate for substantial funds to customize the space. If capital is an issue, landlord and tenant will need to be creative to find a solution.
  • More flexible lease structures: explore shorter terms (for example, 3 to 5 years) if your business outlook is uncertain, or build in options for expansion or contraction.

Due diligence and risk awareness

When a property is at risk of foreclosure or is in special servicing, it’s more than a financial event; it can affect your business operations. Potential risks include:

  • Deferred maintenance or delayed improvements
  • Reduced services (like cleaning, HVAC, or security)
  • Uncertainty during ownership transition, including possible lease renegotiation

If you’re already in a building facing distress, it’s critical to review your lease protections, such as SNDA clauses and remedies if landlord obligations are not met.

For tenants looking for new space, due diligence is paramount:

  • Financial health of the landlord. Go beyond surface-level checks. Investigate the landlord’s overall financial stability, including debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), equity position in the property, and the status of the mortgage (is it current, in special servicing, or nearing maturity?). Look for public filings of lawsuits or foreclosures against the portfolio. A strong cash position indicates a landlord less likely to defer maintenance or services.
  • Property condition. Beyond a walk-through, consider commissioning a third-party Property Condition Assessment (PCA). It can uncover hidden issues with structural integrity, HVAC, plumbing, and electrical systems, especially if the lease shifts repair responsibilities to the tenant. Check for signs of deferred maintenance.
  • Environmental and compliance. Verify compliance with building codes, zoning regulations, and accessibility laws (like the ADA).

Lease protections in a new lease. Make sure your new lease includes critical clauses:

  • A strong SNDA (Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment) protects your lease in the event of foreclosure, allowing you to remain in the space under the same terms if a new owner takes over. Read our SNDA guide.
  • Security protections: negotiate specific language around security or rent abatements in the event of an ownership change or significant disruption.

What to keep in mind

If your space is in a building owned by a distressed landlord (like 535 Fifth Avenue or 500 Seventh Avenue), now is the time to:

  • Scrutinize your lease, especially around services and landlord default
  • Monitor servicing or foreclosure activity
  • Consider asking for security protections in the event of an ownership change
  • If you’re looking for new space, begin your search early: 6 to 18 months before your current lease expires. This allows ample time for due diligence, market research, and negotiation without pressure.
  • Always have a qualified commercial real estate attorney review all lease documents before signing.
